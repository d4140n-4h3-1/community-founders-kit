# 14. Lighting Standard (LED and Daylighting)

## Rule

**Daylight first, LED second.** Where a building has a roof, solar tubes light interiors during the day. **LED is the community standard for all electric lighting.** No incandescent, halogen, or CFL bulbs are bought, donated into service, or installed. Lower power use means smaller solar and wind systems, lower bills, and less heat inside cabins in summer.

## Why It Matters

- LEDs use roughly 75-90% less electricity than incandescent bulbs
- They last many years, so there are fewer replacements and less waste
- They give off very little heat, which keeps cabins cooler and reduces fire risk
- Low draw makes small solar-plus-battery setups workable at Tier 1-2
- Lower loads shrink solar, wind, and sand battery sizing at Tier 3-4

## Tier Fit

- **Tier 0:** Rechargeable LED lanterns, headlamps, and solar string lights. Accept donated LEDs only.
- **Tier 1:** LED market-day lighting and 12V LED strips on a small solar kit. LED security light at the entrance.
- **Tier 2:** LED fixtures in every cabin, the kitchen, and the wash-and-pack station, plus solar tubes in the wash-and-pack station and kitchen. Motion-sensor LEDs at the food store and greenhouse doors. LED grow lights in the seedling greenhouse, on timers.
- **Tier 3:** Solar tubes in every cabin, the Lodge, the Meeting Hall, and the bathhouse. Motion-sensor or timer-controlled LEDs on paths, roads, turnarounds, the pump house, and greenhouse doors. Greenhouse grow lights, if used, are LED only.
- **Tier 4:** Full LED retrofit of any inherited buildings, with a yearly power-use and daylight audit.

## Buying Guidelines

- [ ] Look for an energy-efficiency label (ENERGY STAR, DLC, or your region's equivalent)
- [ ] Warm white (2700-3000K) for living spaces, neutral white (3500-4000K) for work areas
- [ ] Enclosed-rated LEDs in sealed fixtures
- [ ] Dimmable LEDs only with compatible dimmers
- [ ] Outdoor-rated, sealed fixtures for roads, entrances, and greenhouses
- [ ] Porches use amber bug-light LEDs (see [Chapter 15](15-porch-lights-and-garden-spiders.md))
- [ ] Buy in bulk once fixture types are known

## Outdoor and Night Lighting

- Aim lights down and shield them, so they light the path, not the sky
- Use timers or motion sensors so lights are off when no one needs them
- Light the emergency-access entrance and house address so responders can find it
- Put a motion-sensor light on the food store door, the greenhouse doors, and the pump house, positioned so cameras can see ([Chapter 5](../part-1-core-chapters/05-people-and-safeguarding/README.md))

## Daylighting (Solar Tubes)

Tubular daylighting devices, also called solar tubes or sun tunnels, use a roof dome to capture sunlight and a reflective tube to carry it down to a ceiling diffuser. They work like a ceiling light that needs no electricity.

**Why it fits**

- No electricity, so it supports the low-power goal
- Far less heat gain than a skylight, which matters in a metal cabin
- Small roof opening, easy to flash and seal
- Works in windowless rooms: bathhouse, wash-and-pack, storage, kitchen

**Where to use**

- **Tier 2:** wash-and-pack station and shared kitchen (they earn income, so good light matters)
- **Tier 2-3:** one tube per sleeping cabin or main room
- **Tier 3:** the Lodge, the Meeting Hall, bathhouse, storage, and any interior room with no window
- **Food store:** only if the roof opening can be made as secure as the rest of the roof (see [Chapter 9](09-shelter-evolution/README.md))

**Choosing and installing**

- [ ] Size to the room: roughly one 10-14 inch (25-35 cm) tube per 100-150 square feet (10-14 m²)
- [ ] Choose a model with a sealed dome, insulated tube, and a gasketed diffuser, so it doesn't leak heat or condensation
- [ ] Use a flashing kit made for metal roofing, and seal every seam
- [ ] Keep the tube short and straight, since each elbow cuts the light
- [ ] Confirm wind and hail ratings with the manufacturer
- [ ] Add a night-light option: some models take an LED fixture in the same opening
- [ ] Keep the tube out of fire-rated walls and ceilings, or use a rated collar or housing where it must pass through one (see Interior Fire Protection, [Chapter 9](09-shelter-evolution/README.md))

**Cautions**

- Poor flashing is the most common leak, so use a roofer or follow the kit exactly
- A tube is not a substitute for an egress window
- A clear dome can still add summer heat, so choose a low-heat or insulated model
- Insulate the tube through the attic, so it does not create a cold spot or condensation

## Greenhouse Note

Full-spectrum LED grow lights can extend the season for seedlings ([Chapter 7](../part-2-market-engine/07-production-planning/README.md)). Run them on timers during off-peak hours, and count their power in the budget before buying.

## Common Failures

- Buying the cheapest unrated LEDs that flicker or fail within a year
- Using non-enclosed-rated bulbs in sealed fixtures, which overheat
- Mixing color temperatures so rooms look patchy
- Leaving outdoor lights on all night
- Forgetting the LED rule for donated lights and holiday strings
- Skipping the metal-roof flashing kit, so the roof leaks
- Oversizing a solar tube in a small cabin, so it overheats in summer
- Long, bent tubes that deliver almost no light
- No insulation on the attic section of the tube, so condensation drips
- Breaking a fire-rated ceiling with an unsealed tube or fixture

## Money Rules Tie-In

- Track the power bill before and after the LED switch and daylighting, and log the savings
- Use those savings as part of the case for the next tier's solar, wind, or battery funding
- Include the savings in the annual report ([Chapter 8](../part-2-market-engine/08-money-rules/README.md))
