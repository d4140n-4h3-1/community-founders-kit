# 12. Roads and Access

## Principle
Dirt roads become gravel roads as income allows. Good roads protect vehicles, keep emergency services able to reach the site, and get muddy ruts out of market-day routines. Build once, with drainage first, and maintain every season.

## Rule
**Any dirt road on the property is upgraded to gravel.** Upgrade the stretch to the market vehicle and main cabins first, then the rest as funds allow.

## How a Dirt Road Becomes Gravel
1. **Drainage first.** Crown the road (center higher than edges), dig side ditches, and add culverts where water crosses.
2. **Grade and compact.** Level the surface and compact the soil before adding stone.
3. **Fabric (recommended in soft or clay soil).** Geotextile fabric keeps gravel from sinking into mud.
4. **Base layer.** Coarse crushed stone, about 4-6 inches (10-15 cm).
5. **Surface layer.** Smaller crushed stone with fines (road base), about 2-4 inches (5-10 cm).
6. **Compact again.** A plate compactor, or repeated vehicle passes if none is available.
7. **Spare sleeves.** Lay conduit sleeves under the road before graveling, for future water, power, or data lines.

## Tier Fit
- **Tier 0:** Fix the worst mud with ditches, scrap wood, and donated gravel or crushed concrete. Walking paths get mulch or wood chips.
- **Tier 1:** Gravel the market-vehicle route and the parking and loading area.
- **Tier 2:** Gravel all roads to the cabins, wash-and-pack station, food store, and garden access.
- **Tier 3:** Add proper culverts, wider turnarounds, and a loading apron. Where snow drifts, plan snow clearing and drift-reducing windbreaks (Chapter 16).
- **Tier 4:** Maintain on a schedule. Consider paving only the busiest section if it ever makes sense.

## Emergency Access Standards
- [ ] Wide enough for an ambulance or fire truck (confirm with the local fire department)
- [ ] Turnaround or loop at the end, so large vehicles don't have to back out
- [ ] Clear overhead (trim branches)
- [ ] Road name or marker and posted address at the entrance, lit by LED
- [ ] Gate that opens fully, with a key lockbox or a code shared with emergency services (a locked gate must never block an emergency vehicle)
- [ ] Boundary and "no trespassing" signs at the entrance, in black with yellow stenciled letters, under the local sign rules (Chapter 5)
- [ ] Site address and directions posted with the emergency contacts (Chapter 5)

## Maintenance
- Re-grade and add gravel each spring and after heavy storms
- Keep ditches and culverts clear of leaves and debris
- Fill ruts and potholes early
- Fix the cause of washouts (usually water) instead of just refilling with gravel

## Materials and Cost
- **Cheapest:** crushed concrete, recycled asphalt millings, or local quarry "road base"
- Ask quarries, contractors, and the local road department about leftover loads or discounts
- Buy in truckloads, not bags
- Spread the work over seasons, muddiest section first

## Common Failures
- Dumping gravel in a wet spot with no drainage
- Skipping compaction
- Using round "pea gravel," which never packs hard
- Blocking natural water flow and flooding a neighbor or a garden
- Making the road too narrow for emergency vehicles
- Ignoring a washout until it cuts the road in half
- A locked gate with no way in for responders

> Check local rules for culverts, drainage changes, and driveway access onto public roads, since some require permits. In clay-heavy soil, fabric and drainage matter most.
