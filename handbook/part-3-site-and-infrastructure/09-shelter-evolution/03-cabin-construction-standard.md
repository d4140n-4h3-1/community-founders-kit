# Cabin Construction Standard

**Rule:** **Every cabin has a noncombustible shell. Steel is the minimum, and filled concrete block (CMU) is an approved upgrade.** No wood-framed or other combustible-shell cabins. Check local code, which may allow or require other materials, and have an engineer or builder confirm the design.

**What "noncombustible" does and doesn't mean**
- Steel and block don't feed a fire, which helps against wildfire embers and spread between buildings
- **Steel can still fail in a hot fire,** and insulation, finishes, and contents still burn, so detectors, exits, and clearances are never skipped
- Fire safety rules are unchanged: smoke and CO detectors, egress from every bedroom, extinguishers, and vented heaters only
- Use fire-rated or noncombustible roofing and vents, and ember-resistant screens, where wildfire is a risk ([Chapter 16](../16-wind-power-and-wind-safety.md))

**Steel vs. block**
| | Steel cabin (minimum) | Block cabin (option) |
|---|---|---|
| Cost and speed | Lower cost, fast, often a kit | Higher cost, slower, volunteer-buildable course by course |
| Heat and cold | Heats and cools fast, so insulation and shade matter most | High thermal mass evens out temperature swings, with insulation still needed |
| Strength | Needs anchoring against wind | Very strong when cores are filled and reinforced |
| Moisture | Condensation if not insulated | Damp-proofing and drainage needed |
| Pests and security | Good if sealed | Excellent: hard to chew or force |
| Second story | Often needs engineered framing | Needs engineered floor and foundation |
| Future changes | Easy to modify or move | Harder to alter once built |

**Block cabin rules**
- [ ] **Fill cores with rebar and grout** at engineered spacing. Unfilled block can crack or break through (as in the food store and trenches).
- [ ] **Foundation sized for block weight,** with footings below frost depth where freezing occurs, and drainage so water doesn't sit against the walls
- [ ] **Damp-proof the outside,** seal joints, and use flashing and a proper roof overhang
- [ ] **Insulate** with rigid foam or similar, outside the block where possible, so the mass stays inside the thermal envelope
- [ ] **Seismic and wind design:** an engineer confirms reinforcement for the local conditions
- [ ] **Roof:** steel or other noncombustible roof, anchored to the walls with engineered ties
- [ ] **Egress:** every bedroom still has its own window or exit, with lintels sized for the openings
- [ ] **Run conduit and plumbing in place before grouting,** or in the furring cavity, since cutting finished block is hard and weakens it
- [ ] **Radon and ventilation:** test for radon and plan fresh-air ventilation, since tight masonry and insulation hold air in
- [ ] **Same limits as any cabin:** two stories plus a short attic, no more than four residents, and the family-cabin layout rules
- [ ] **Interior finished with drywall on furring,** as set out in Interior Fire Protection below

**Tier fit**
- **Tier 2:** steel cabins remain the default first housing, since they are faster and cheaper. Block cabins only if the budget, a mason or builder, and the permit are in place.
- **Tier 3:** block cabins become a practical option for new housing, and the Lodge and Meeting Hall may be block
- **Tier 4:** long-term homes may be block, with solar, better windows, and thermal mass in the design

**Climate notes**
- **Hot-dry (Arizona-type):** block's thermal mass helps with big day-night swings. Shade and a reflective roof still matter most.
- **Cold (Montana-type):** keep insulation continuous and outside the mass. Protect foundations from frost heave, and manage condensation.
- **Wildfire zones:** noncombustible walls and roofs help, but keep defensible space and ember-proof vents.

**Common failures**
- Assuming "steel doesn't burn" means no fire precautions
- Block walls with empty cores, or no reinforcement
- No damp-proofing, so moisture wicks through walls and causes mold
- Insulating inside only, losing the benefit of thermal mass
- Forgetting conduit and plumbing before grouting, then cutting walls open
- Foundation sized for a steel kit but used for block
- Building block before the budget, a mason, and the permit are ready

**Funding notes**
- Block is slower and costs more per cabin, but it lasts longer and needs little upkeep
- Surplus block, volunteer labor, and local mason training can lower the cost
- The food store and utility trenches already use block, so skills and supply can be shared

> Building codes, energy rules, and wildfire standards vary by location. Confirm materials, fire ratings, and reinforcement with the local building office and an engineer before you build.
