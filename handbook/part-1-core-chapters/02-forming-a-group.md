# 2. Forming a Group

## Principle
**A community starts as a group of people who trust each other enough to write things down.** Land, tents, and gardens come second. Spend the first weeks agreeing on purpose, roles, money, and what happens when things go wrong, while everyone is still friends. The handbook works for any group willing to try, so start with whoever is actually ready, not whoever you wish was.

## Start With Purpose
- [ ] Write a **one-paragraph purpose**: who the community is for, what it offers, and what it will not do
- [ ] Write **three to five shared values** in plain words (for example: honesty with money, respect, safety first)
- [ ] Decide what "success" looks like after the first season, in numbers where possible (people housed, harvests sold, costs covered)
- [ ] Keep it short enough to say out loud in 30 seconds, and put it at the top of the charter (Chapter 3)

## Finding the Founding Group
- **Aim for 5-10 committed people.** Fewer is fragile, and more is hard to coordinate before the rules exist.
- Good places to look: community gardens, churches and faith groups, mutual aid networks, homesteading and permaculture groups, volunteer and neighborhood organizations, and local farm or extension programs
- Be honest in every invitation about what is offered, what is expected, and that the community is starting small. Overpromising causes the worst departures.
- Invite people with a mix of skills (see below), not only close friends
- Ask each person for time and effort before money
- Welcome people at the pace the group can absorb, and keep a short waiting list (Chapter 5)

## Skills and Roles
Take a quick inventory of who can do what.
- **Growing:** gardening, soil, irrigation, seedlings
- **Building and repair:** carpentry, block work, drywall, plumbing, electrical, tools
- **Money and records:** bookkeeping, cash handling, tax paperwork, minutes and archive keeping
- **People:** mediation, first aid, safeguarding, teaching, cooking
- **Market and outreach:** selling, talking to neighbors, writing, social media
- **Legal and liaison:** dealing with officials, landowners, and the market manager

Then name roles with **two people for every critical task**: cash, keys, filings, safeguarding, first aid, camera footage, the meeting archive, and each main crop (Chapters 5, 7, 8, and 9). Give roles a term and a handover, so no job belongs to one person forever.

## The Honest Conversation
Before anyone commits, talk through these topics and write down the answers. Disagreement now is cheap, and disagreement after move-in is not.
- **Time:** how many hours a week each person can really give (the starting guideline is about 12, Chapter 5)
- **Money:** who pays for what, who holds the money, and what happens if the money runs out
- **Housing:** who lives on site, in what, and for how long
- **Children and vulnerable people:** who may be present, and the safeguarding rules (Chapter 5)
- **Substances and weapons:** the group's policy, within local law (Chapter 5)
- **Beliefs:** the community is neutral and treats everyone equally, with shared halls open to all (Chapter 3)
- **Privacy and security:** what is recorded, where, and who may see it, including whether open meetings are recorded for an archive (Chapters 5 and 9)
- **Decisions:** how choices are made (see below) and who can veto what
- **Conflict:** the ladder from a direct talk to outside mediation (Chapter 5)
- **Leaving:** how someone exits, and what happens to their share of work, money, and belongings
- **Failure:** what the group does if the pilot does not work

## How the Group Decides
Pick a method and write it down. A mix often works best.
- **Everyday decisions:** the person responsible decides within agreed limits, and reports back
- **Shared decisions:** discuss, then reach agreement by consent ("can everyone live with this?") or a simple majority
- **Big decisions** (money above a set amount, new members, land, changes to the charter): a larger majority, a cooling-off period, and a written record
- **Stuck decisions:** a trial run with a review date, or a neutral facilitator
- Keep short minutes of every meeting, with decisions and who does what by when. **Written minutes are always the official record,** even if a meeting is also recorded (Chapter 9).

## Money and Trust From the Start
- Open a **dedicated community account with two signers**. Never mix personal and community money (Chapter 8).
- Record every contribution and expense, and give receipts
- **No one hands over savings, identity documents, or property as the price of joining.** A community that asks for these is a warning sign.
- Never let one person hold all the money, keys, and decisions
- Treat early contributions as **voluntary and recorded**, and ask an accountant how they are treated before collecting regularly (Chapter 8)

## Choosing a Starting Legal Form
Rules differ by place, so ask an attorney and an accountant before the group collects money, signs a lease, or holds an event (Chapter 4). Common starting points, in order of simplicity:
| Option | How it works | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Partner with an existing organization | A church, nonprofit, or land trust hosts the pilot, or acts as fiscal sponsor and handles money and insurance | Written agreement on who is responsible for what |
| Simple association or club | A written agreement among members, sometimes registered locally | Members may be personally liable, so check insurance and rules |
| Early incorporation | Form a nonprofit or other entity from the start | Costs, filings, and rules begin sooner |

The handbook assumes a nonprofit or land trust by Tier 3 (Chapter 8). Until then, make sure the money, the insurance, and the land agreement all have a clear legal home, and keep the board plan in mind: include at least some non-resident advisors early if possible.

## The Founding Agreement
Put the following in one short document that every founder signs (template in Appendix B1).
- [ ] Purpose and values
- [ ] Roles and who backs them up
- [ ] Decision method and meeting rhythm
- [ ] How money is held, spent, and recorded
- [ ] The conduct rules and conflict ladder (Chapter 5)
- [ ] How people join, how they leave, and how someone can be asked to leave fairly
- [ ] What happens to money and property if the group dissolves
- [ ] A review date, so the agreement can change as the group learns

This becomes the first draft of the charter (Chapter 3), and an attorney should review it before Tier 1.

## First Meeting Agenda
1. Welcome, and share why each person is here
2. Read and adjust the purpose and values
3. Walk through the honest conversation list
4. Name roles and backups
5. Agree on the decision method and meeting schedule
6. Decide who will contact the accountant, the market manager, and a landowner or host (Chapters 4 and 6)
7. Set the next three actions, with names and dates

## The First 90 Days
- **Weeks 1-2:** hold the first meeting, draft the founding agreement, and open the account
- **Weeks 3-6:** consult the accountant and attorney, check local rules, and start the compliance calendar (Chapter 8)
- **Weeks 6-10:** talk with possible landowners or hosts, ask the market manager about vendor rules, and start a small garden or container beds
- **Weeks 10-13:** hold a review, adjust the agreement, and decide together whether to continue, adjust, or pause

## Tier Fit
- **Tier 0:** the founding group of 5-10, a written purpose, founding agreement, two-signer account, roles with backups, and a regular meeting with written minutes
- **Tier 1:** a trial period for newcomers, a safeguarding lead and deputy, and the charter adopted by all members (Chapter 5)
- **Tier 2:** a written process for joining and leaving that an attorney has reviewed, and the first season review
- **Tier 3:** the nonprofit or land trust formed, with a board that includes non-residents
- **Tier 4:** mentor new groups, and add lessons from your founding years to the handbook

## Warning Signs
- One charismatic person makes every decision and controls money, keys, and information
- People are pressured to join quickly, give money, or cut ties with family and friends
- Questions about money or rules are treated as disloyalty
- Rules apply to newcomers but not to founders
- Members are asked to sign over property, benefits, or identity documents
- Secrets are kept "to protect the community"

If you see these, pause, bring in an outside person (a mediator, advisor, or attorney), and follow the conflict and safeguarding steps in Chapter 5.

## Common Failures
- Buying land or tents before agreeing on rules
- Skipping the honest conversation, then arguing about money or children later
- Recruiting only close friends, so skills are missing
- One founder holding all the money, keys, and decisions
- No plan for what happens if someone leaves or the group ends
- Taking money from members with no records or receipts
- Overpromising to recruits, then losing them in the first season
- No review date, so a weak agreement never improves
- Growing the group faster than water, septic, and leadership can support

## Money and Legal Tie-In
- Keep a simple log of contributions, expenses, and volunteer hours from day one (Chapter 8 and Appendix D)
- Ask the accountant before the first sale or regular contribution, and the attorney before the first lease or fee
- Get insurance in place before visitors, events, or any market sales (Chapters 4 and 6)

> Group formation, liability, and fundraising rules differ widely by location. This handbook gives no legal or tax advice, so have an attorney and an accountant review the founding agreement and starting legal form before the group collects money or signs anything.
