# Daily Life: Chores, Shared Spaces, and Routines

**Principle:** **Daily life runs on small, fair routines.** Most resentment in a community comes from unseen work, unclear shared spaces, and rules nobody wrote down. Make the work visible, share it fairly, keep shared spaces simple, and leave room for rest and joy. Routines should serve people, not the other way around.

**Sharing the work**
- [ ] Write a **chore list** covering everything the community needs: cooking, cleaning, water and sanitation checks, trash and compost, garden and greenhouse checks, tool care, market prep, and admin (and animal care, if animals are ever kept)
- [ ] **Guideline: about 12 hours a week per adult member.** This is the starting figure, and the group may change it in the charter after a season of experience.
- [ ] Count **all work** toward it, including care work (cooking, childcare help, checking on elders), repairs, admin, and essential tasks
- [ ] **Adjust the expectation in writing, the same way for everyone,** for children, parents' childcare, elders, illness, injury, disability, and people with jobs off site
- [ ] Split chores by **rotation** for the unpleasant jobs and by **skill or preference** for the rest
- [ ] Post a **weekly chore chart** with names and a backup for each task (Appendix A11)
- [ ] Record hours in the volunteer-hours log (Appendix D7), and review them monthly: kindly, not as a punishment
- [ ] If someone is well below the guideline for a while, the first step is **a caring conversation** about what is getting in the way. Nothing happens to their housing or membership because of hours alone.
- [ ] Only repeated, unaddressed problems go up the conflict ladder, under the same fair process as any other issue

**Legal check:** an hours expectation tied to housing can be treated as rent or even as employment in some places. Have an attorney confirm how to word it, and make sure it never conflicts with the rule above that no one is pressured into work in exchange for housing. Frame it as a shared membership guideline, set in the charter, never as payment for a bed.

**Fairness rules**
- No one does a task only because they are "good at it" or "never complain"
- Skipped chores are raised early and kindly, not stored up
- Illness, injury, and family emergencies pause duties without blame
- Newcomers get a clear job list, a buddy, and a gentle start during the trial period
- Hardship cases follow written rules, the same for everyone (Chapter 8)
- Leaders do their share of the ordinary chores, too

**Essential tasks and the rest day**
Rest days pause ordinary work, not essential care. Some things cannot wait a day.
- **Essential tasks** are: checking drinking water and toilets, the morning and evening greenhouse check (vents, doors, temperature, and water), frost cover or heating in cold, caring for anyone who is ill, and emergency checks (plus feeding and watering animals, once animals exist)
- Write the essential-task list, and rotate it so **no one person works every rest day**
- Anyone who covers essential tasks on a rest day takes **time off another day that week**
- Essential tasks count toward the 12-hour guideline
- Keep essential tasks short and simple, so a rest day still feels like rest

**Shared spaces**
Common spaces are the heart of daily life, and the place where small conflicts begin.
- **The Lodge (social hall):** open to everyone on equal terms, booked fairly, with no cameras (Chapter 9)
- **The Meeting Hall:** governance meetings, with a recording system under its own policy (Chapter 9)
- **Shared kitchen:** posted cleaning rules, labeled food shelves, dishes washed before bed, food stored sealed, and hygiene rules for anyone cooking for others (Chapters 6 and 10)
- **Meeting and meal space** before the halls exist: covered, with seating, light, and a safe heater or fan (Chapter 9)
- **Bathhouse and toilets:** cleaned on rota, stocked daily, with privacy and lighting (Chapter 10)
- **Laundry and drying:** a schedule, so water and power are not used all at once
- **Storage and tool shed:** locked, with a sign-out list for shared tools and a place for each item
- **Children's areas:** away from hazards, visible to adults
- **Quiet space:** one spot where anyone can sit alone or rest
- **Common rules for all spaces:** leave it cleaner than you found it, report breakages the same day, and put things back

**Privacy and personal space**
- Every household has a **private home,** with its own door and key, where it can
- Nobody enters another person's space without permission, except in a real emergency
- Personal belongings stay personal, and borrowing needs a "yes"
- Visitors in private spaces follow the visitor rules
- People may decline to share personal or health information, including their beliefs

**Meals and food**
- Decide how meals work: **shared meals** on set days, individual cooking, or a mix
- Share **food costs and groceries** by a written method, and record them (Chapter 8)
- Handle **diets and allergies** respectfully: ask, label, and keep allergen safety in the kitchen
- Never serve unsafe food to save money: keep cold food cold, cook thoroughly, and wash hands
- Use the **community food share** from the gardens first (Chapter 7), and log what is used
- Send surplus to the market or a food bank, not to waste

**Quiet hours and noise**
- Set **quiet hours** in writing (for example, evenings and early mornings), shorter for market and harvest days
- Keep generators, power tools, and loud music to agreed hours and places
- Think of neighbors outside the community as well
- Raise noise problems with a calm direct talk first

**Visitors, guests, and community days**
- Follow the visitor rules (sign in, host responsible, overnight limits)
- Hold an **open day** each season for neighbors and officials (Chapter 8)
- Plan **volunteer days** with a clear task list, safety briefing, and a first-aider on site
- Give visitors a **short welcome sheet**: where toilets are, where not to go, how to reach a host, and what to do in an emergency (Appendix A12)

**Pets**
- Decide in writing which pets are allowed, how many, and where
- Owners are responsible for feeding, water, waste, vaccinations, and keeping animals away from food, market gardens, greenhouses, and the food store
- Keep pets away from market produce and the wash-and-pack station (Chapter 7)
- Check local rules, and keep dogs leashed or fenced around visitors and children
- Wildlife is not fed, and food is stored sealed

**Animal care (optional, Tier 3 or later, only if the community chooses)**
**The default is no animals.** The community adds farm or other working animals only after a group vote, and only once these are true:
- [ ] The community has reached Tier 3, with a stable budget and the reserve in place
- [ ] A named animal-care lead and a backup are trained, with a written care sheet (Appendix A13)
- [ ] A daily rota puts two people on every day, rest days and illness included
- [ ] Feed, shelter, fencing, and vet costs are in the budget (Chapter 8)
- [ ] Local rules are checked, and the disaster plan includes the animals (Appendix D15)
- [ ] Animals are kept away from produce, the wash-and-pack station, the food store, and drinking water (Chapters 7 and 10)
- [ ] Manure is composted before it goes near food crops (Chapter 7), and any sale of meat, eggs, or dairy follows strict food rules (Chapter 6)

**Once animals are kept, they are fed, watered, and checked every day, with no exceptions for rest days, holidays, weather, or illness.** Water comes first, with shade in heat and thawed water in freezing weather. Add one species at a time, starting small. If the group can no longer care for them, it rehomes them responsibly.

**Waste, trash, and recycling**
- Set up **trash, recycling, and compost** with clear bins and a collection plan
- Keep food waste in sealed bins, or in a compost system placed away from wells and the septic field (Chapter 7)
- Never burn trash, and follow local rules for open fires
- Store hazardous waste (paint, fuel, chemicals) locked and away from food and children, and take it to a proper disposal site
- Do a **weekly walk-round** for litter and hazards

**Rest, joy, and wellbeing**
- **Schedule rest:** at least one day each week with no ordinary assigned work, for everyone, with essential tasks covered by rota
- Plan shared **meals, music, games, or celebrations**, so the community is more than a workplace
- Mark **harvests, new arrivals, and farewells** together
- Watch for loneliness, low mood, and burnout, and check in gently
- Make it easy to ask for help, and to say "I need a break"
- Keep a list of local support services, and know the crisis line

**Weekly rhythm** *(a sample to adapt)*
| Day | Example |
|---|---|
| Monday | Informal check-in (15-30 minutes, unrecorded), chore chart review |
| Tuesday-Wednesday | Garden work, repairs, seedling care |
| Thursday | Harvest, wash, and pack for market |
| Friday or Saturday | Market day, two-person cash count |
| Sunday | Rest day. Shared meal, quiet time. Essential tasks only, covered by rota. |
| Every day | Morning and evening greenhouse check |
| Monthly | Governance meeting in the Meeting Hall, budget review, food-store, greenhouse, and camera inspection, chore chart review |
| Each season | Review, open day, safety drill |

**Meetings that work**
- Keep the **weekly check-in short and informal**: what is going well, what needs help, and who is doing what. It is not recorded.
- Hold decisions, votes, and reviews in a **governance meeting** with an agenda, a timekeeper, and written minutes (Chapter 2), in the Meeting Hall once it exists
- Let everyone speak, including the quiet ones
- Settle small matters outside the meeting, and bring only shared decisions
- Never hold conflict hearings or personal criticism in an open meeting
