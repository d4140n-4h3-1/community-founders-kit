# Daily Life: Chores, Shared Spaces, and Routines

**Principle:** **Daily life runs on small, fair routines.** Most resentment in a community comes from unseen work, unclear shared spaces, and rules nobody wrote down. Make the work visible, share it fairly, keep shared spaces simple, and leave room for rest and joy. Routines should serve people, not the other way around.

**Sharing the work**

- [ ] Write a **chore list** covering everything the community needs: cooking, cleaning, water and sanitation checks, trash and compost, garden and greenhouse checks, tool care, market prep, and admin (and animal care, if animals are ever kept)
- [ ] **Guideline: about 12 hours a week per adult member.** This is the starting figure, and the group may change it in the charter after a season of experience.
- [ ] Count **all work** toward it, including care work (cooking, childcare help, checking on elders), repairs, admin, and essential tasks
- [ ] **Adjust the expectation in writing, the same way for everyone,** for children, parents' childcare, elders, illness, injury, disability, and people with jobs off site
- [ ] Split chores by **rotation** for the unpleasant jobs and by **skill or preference** for the rest
- [ ] Post a **weekly chore chart** with names and a backup for each task ([Appendix A11](../../appendices/appendix-a-checklists/a11-weekly-chore-chart.md))
- [ ] Record hours in the volunteer-hours log ([Appendix D7](../../appendices/appendix-d-logs-sheets-and-reports/d07-contribution-and-volunteer-hours-log.md)), and review them monthly: kindly, not as a punishment. Before each season, check that the planned work fits the hours available ([Appendix D21](../../appendices/appendix-d-logs-sheets-and-reports/d21-labor-budget.md)).
- [ ] If someone is well below the guideline for a while, the first step is **a caring conversation** about what is getting in the way. Nothing happens to their housing or membership because of hours alone.
- [ ] Only repeated, unaddressed problems go up the conflict ladder, under the same fair process as any other issue

**Legal check:** an hours expectation tied to housing can be treated as rent or even as employment in some places. Have an attorney confirm how to word it, and make sure it never conflicts with the rule above that no one is pressured into work in exchange for housing. Frame it as a shared membership guideline, set in the charter, never as payment for a bed.

**Fairness rules**

- No one does a task only because they are "good at it" or "never complain"
- Skipped chores are raised early and kindly, not stored up
- Illness, injury, and family emergencies pause duties without blame
- Newcomers get a clear job list, a buddy, and a gentle start during the trial period
- Hardship cases follow written rules, the same for everyone ([Chapter 8](../../part-2-market-engine/08-money-rules/README.md))
- Leaders do their share of the ordinary chores, too

**Essential tasks and the rest day**
Rest days pause ordinary work, not essential care. Some things cannot wait a day.

- **Essential tasks** are: checking drinking water and toilets, the morning and evening greenhouse check (vents, doors, temperature, and water), frost cover or heating in cold, caring for anyone who is ill, and emergency checks (plus feeding and watering animals, once animals exist)
- Write the essential-task list, and rotate it so **no one person works every rest day**
- Anyone who covers essential tasks on a rest day takes **time off another day that week**
- Essential tasks count toward the 12-hour guideline
- Keep essential tasks short and simple, so a rest day still feels like rest

**Shared spaces**
Common spaces are the heart of daily life, and the place where small conflicts begin.

- **The Lodge (social hall):** open to everyone on equal terms, booked fairly, with no cameras ([Chapter 9](../../part-3-site-and-infrastructure/09-shelter-evolution/README.md))
- **The Meeting Hall:** governance meetings, with a recording system under its own policy ([Chapter 9](../../part-3-site-and-infrastructure/09-shelter-evolution/README.md))
- **Shared kitchen:** posted cleaning rules, labeled food shelves, dishes washed before bed, food stored sealed, and hygiene rules for anyone cooking for others (Chapters [6](../../part-2-market-engine/06-market-business-basics/README.md) and [10](../../part-3-site-and-infrastructure/10-water-plumbing-and-septic/README.md))
- **Meeting and meal space** before the halls exist: covered, with seating, light, and a safe heater or fan ([Chapter 9](../../part-3-site-and-infrastructure/09-shelter-evolution/README.md))
- **Bathhouse and toilets:** cleaned on rota, stocked daily, with privacy and lighting ([Chapter 10](../../part-3-site-and-infrastructure/10-water-plumbing-and-septic/README.md))
- **Laundry and drying:** a schedule, so water and power are not used all at once
- **Storage and tool shed:** locked, with a sign-out list for shared tools and a place for each item
- **Children's areas:** away from hazards, visible to adults
- **Quiet space:** one spot where anyone can sit alone or rest
- **Common rules for all spaces:** leave it cleaner than you found it, report breakages the same day, and put things back

**Privacy and personal space**

- Every household has a **private home,** with its own door and key, where it can
- Nobody enters another person's space without permission, except in a real emergency
- Personal belongings stay personal, and borrowing needs a "yes"
- Visitors in private spaces follow the visitor rules
- People may decline to share personal or health information, including their beliefs

**Meals and food**

- Decide how meals work: **shared meals** on set days, individual cooking, or a mix
- Share **food costs and groceries** by a written method, and record them ([Chapter 8](../../part-2-market-engine/08-money-rules/README.md))
- Handle **diets and allergies** respectfully: ask, label, and keep allergen safety in the kitchen
- Never serve unsafe food to save money: keep cold food cold, cook thoroughly, and wash hands
- Use the **community food share** from the gardens first ([Chapter 7](../../part-2-market-engine/07-production-planning/README.md)), and log what is used
- Send surplus to the market or a food bank, not to waste

**Quiet hours and noise**

- Set **quiet hours** in writing (for example, evenings and early mornings), shorter for market and harvest days
- Keep generators, power tools, and loud music to agreed hours and places
- Think of neighbors outside the community as well
- Raise noise problems with a calm direct talk first

**Visitors, guests, and community days**

- Follow the visitor rules (sign in, host responsible, overnight limits)
- Hold an **open day** each season for neighbors and officials ([Chapter 8](../../part-2-market-engine/08-money-rules/README.md))
- Plan **volunteer days** with a clear task list, safety briefing, and a first-aider on site
- Give visitors a **short welcome sheet**: where toilets are, where not to go, how to reach a host, and what to do in an emergency ([Appendix A12](../../appendices/appendix-a-checklists/a12-visitor-welcome-sheet.md))

**Pets**

- Decide in writing which pets are allowed, how many, and where
- Owners are responsible for feeding, water, waste, vaccinations, and keeping animals away from food, market gardens, greenhouses, and the food store
- Keep pets away from market produce and the wash-and-pack station ([Chapter 7](../../part-2-market-engine/07-production-planning/README.md))
- Check local rules, and keep dogs leashed or fenced around visitors and children
- Guardian dogs that live with the livestock follow the **Guardian animals** rules below
- Wildlife is not fed, and food is stored sealed

**Animal care (optional, Tier 3 or later, only if the community chooses)**
**The default is no animals.** The community adds farm or other working animals only after a group vote, and only once these are true:

- [ ] The community has reached Tier 3, with a stable budget and the reserve in place
- [ ] A named animal-care lead and a backup are trained, with a written care sheet ([Appendix A13](../../appendices/appendix-a-checklists/a13-animal-care-sheet.md))
- [ ] A daily rota puts two people on every day, rest days and illness included
- [ ] Feed, shelter, fencing, and vet costs are in the budget ([Chapter 8](../../part-2-market-engine/08-money-rules/README.md))
- [ ] Local rules are checked, and the disaster plan includes the animals ([Appendix D15](../../appendices/appendix-d-logs-sheets-and-reports/d15-disaster-plan-template.md))
- [ ] A predator plan is written: prevention first, and any farm guns under the Firearms Safety rules
- [ ] Animals are kept away from produce, the wash-and-pack station, the food store, and drinking water (Chapters [7](../../part-2-market-engine/07-production-planning/README.md) and [10](../../part-3-site-and-infrastructure/10-water-plumbing-and-septic/README.md))
- [ ] Manure is composted before it goes near food crops ([Chapter 7](../../part-2-market-engine/07-production-planning/README.md)), and any sale of meat, eggs, or dairy follows strict food rules ([Chapter 6](../../part-2-market-engine/06-market-business-basics/README.md))
- [ ] Intact males are housed apart from females until the group votes to breed (see **Breeding** below)

**Once animals are kept, they are fed, watered, and checked every day, with no exceptions for rest days, holidays, weather, or illness.** Water comes first, with shade in heat and thawed water in freezing weather. Add one species at a time, starting small. If the group can no longer care for them, it rehomes them responsibly.

**Breeding**

**Rule:** intact (not neutered) males are always kept apart from females until the group decides that breeding is needed. Neutered males, such as wether goats, may live with the females, since they can't breed.

- [ ] **Separate housing for every intact male,** with fencing he can't climb, jump, or breed through. Leave a gap or a double fence between his pen and the females'.
- [ ] **Company where the species needs it,** such as a wether to keep a buck goat or a ram company. Roosters may fight each other, so watch any that share a pen.
- [ ] **Young males are separated before they can breed:** buck kids by about 8 weeks old and ram lambs by about 3 months old, or sooner if the vet advises, and young roosters as soon as they can be told apart from the hens
- [ ] **Breeding happens only under a written plan the group votes on:** why it's needed (such as replacing older hens, or kids so the does give milk), how many young are expected, where they will live, the cost, the vet's advice, and what happens to the young males born
- [ ] **Often the simplest choice is not to keep a male at all:** borrow or hire a buck for the breeding season, use artificial insemination through a vet, or buy fertile eggs or young chicks from a trusted, disease-tested source
- [ ] **Breeding season:** bucks in season smell strongly and can be aggressive, and rams can charge without warning, so two adults handle them, no one turns their back on a ram, and children stay away
- [ ] **Records:** each breeding and birth goes on the [animal care sheet](../../appendices/appendix-a-checklists/a13-animal-care-sheet.md)

**Identifying animals**

**Rule:** no ear tags. They hurt when they go in, and they can catch on fences and brush and tear the ear.

- [ ] **Know each animal** by name, photos, and markings, recorded on the [animal care sheet](../../appendices/appendix-a-checklists/a13-animal-care-sheet.md)
- [ ] **Goats and sheep:** a breakaway collar with a name tag, one that snaps open if it catches, checked weekly for fit
- [ ] **Chickens:** soft leg bands, checked as the birds grow so they never get tight
- [ ] **Where the law requires official ID,** as many places do for sheep and goats that are sold, moved, or shown, ask the vet or the agriculture authority which methods are approved, and use one that isn't an ear tag. If an ear tag is the only approved method, don't do what requires it: keep the flock closed, and buy and sell only where no tag is needed.
- [ ] **Animals that arrive already tagged keep their official tags,** since removing official ID is often illegal. Check those ears weekly, and call the vet if a tag catches or an ear is sore.

**Guardian animals**

**Rule:** a guardian animal is raised with the animals it will protect, starting young, and proves itself before it guards alone. Choose it by the individual animal and the local predators, not by reputation alone.

**Raise them as pups**

- [ ] **Bring a guardian dog home as a puppy,** usually at about 8-12 weeks old, and raise it with the livestock it will guard, so it bonds with them early. The earlier it is socialized, the better.
- [ ] **Socialize it with people too:** residents, children under supervision, regular visitors, and the vet, so it accepts handling and knows who belongs
- [ ] **Teach the basics:** coming when called, staying inside the fence, and accepting a collar, a leash, and a vet's exam
- [ ] **Supervise it as it grows.** Young dogs play rough and can hurt livestock without meaning to, and many don't settle until they are about two years old. Correct rough play early, and don't leave a young dog alone with the animals until it has proven itself.

**Choosing a guardian**

1. **A dog raised from a pup with the community's own animals,** trained and proven over time. Any breed may be raised for the job, including pit bulls, which some communities raise and train with great success.
2. **For a community new to guardian dogs, or facing bears, cougars, or wolves,** the livestock guardian breeds (such as the Great Pyrenees, Anatolian Shepherd, Maremma, Akbash, and Kangal) are the safest default, since they were bred to guard alone around the clock.
3. **A donkey,** where the threat is mainly coyotes, foxes, and stray dogs (see **Donkeys** below)

**Proving it before it guards alone**

- [ ] **Introduce the dog to each kind of animal** it will guard, under supervision. Goats and sheep run and bolt in a way chickens don't, which can start a chase even in a dog that is calm with hens.
- [ ] **A trial of a few months,** from supervised visits to longer unsupervised stretches, with no chasing, nipping, or killing. A trainer or the vet helps judge it.
- [ ] **Off guard duty at the first attack** on livestock or a person, until a trainer or the vet has assessed the dog again

**Rules for every guardian dog**

- [ ] **Allowed by local law and the insurer.** Many places restrict pit bull-type dogs and some other breeds, and the community's insurance must cover the dog.
- [ ] **Licensed and vaccinated** as the law requires, including rabies, with vet care, food, water, and shelter in the animal budget and the daily rota
- [ ] **Spayed or neutered,** unless the group's breeding plan says otherwise (see **Breeding** above)
- [ ] **Safe with children and visitors,** under the safeguarding and pet rules, and visitors are told where the guardian dogs work and not to enter their pens or feed them ([visitor welcome sheet](../../appendices/appendix-a-checklists/a12-visitor-welcome-sheet.md))
- [ ] **Stays on the land.** Fencing keeps the dog in, since a guardian that roams can be a danger to neighbors and a legal liability.
- [ ] **Neighbors know** that barking at night is how a guardian warns off predators
- [ ] **Guards the animals, never against people.** A guardian dog is never used to threaten or chase trespassers. People are handled under the outside harassment response plan ([Outside Harassment and Community Security](06-outside-harassment-and-community-security.md)).

**Donkeys**

- **Effective** against coyotes, foxes, and stray dogs, which donkeys dislike and will chase, stomp, and sometimes kill
- **Not enough** against bears, cougars, or wolf packs, which can hurt or kill a donkey
- [ ] **Jennies (females) or geldings (neutered males) only.** Never a jack (intact male), which can be dangerous to lambs, kids, and people.
- [ ] **One donkey per flock or pasture** is the usual starting point, so the donkey bonds with the flock and treats it as its herd
- [ ] **A bonded pair can guard too,** keeping each other company while they protect a large flock, or two pastures side by side with one donkey in each. Introduce both to the flock slowly, and watch that they guard it rather than wandering off together or handling young animals roughly.
- [ ] **Donkeys suffer alone.** A single guardian donkey's flock must be its company, with daily attention from people. Where no guarding is needed, keep donkeys in pairs.
- [ ] **Kept apart from dogs.** Donkeys often attack dogs, guardian dogs included, so a flock has a guardian dog or a donkey, not both.
- [ ] **Introduce it slowly,** across a fence for a few weeks, before it joins the flock
- [ ] **Care:** hoof trimming, teeth checked by the vet, shelter from rain, and limited rich pasture, since donkeys put on weight easily and can founder (a painful hoof disease)

**Goats for brush and grass (optional, under the animal rules above)**

Goats are browsers more than grazers. They eat grass, but they prefer brush, weeds, vines, and young shrubs, which makes them very good at clearing overgrown ground. For keeping pasture grass short and even, sheep or a mower do the job better (see **Sheep** below).

**Where they help most**

- Defensible space and fire breaks around buildings ([Chapter 16](../../part-3-site-and-infrastructure/16-wind-power-and-wind-safety.md))
- The second way out and the fence lines ([Two Ways Out](../../part-3-site-and-infrastructure/12-roads-and-access.md#two-ways-out))
- Steep or rough ground a mower can't reach, and tough brush such as blackberry or poison ivy

**Two ways to use them**

- **Hire a targeted grazing service,** whose owners bring the goats, fencing, and water for a few days or weeks. The service owns and cares for the animals, so a community may hire one at any tier, with the landowner's permission, a written contract covering care, fencing, water, and liability, and proof that the service is insured.
- **Keep a small herd** under the animal rules above: Tier 3 or later, a group vote, a care lead and a backup, a daily rota, and a budget.

**Keeping goats**

- [ ] **Never just one.** Goats are herd animals and suffer alone. Start with two or three, such as does or neutered males, which are calm and common for brush work. Any intact buck lives apart from the does (see **Breeding** above).
- [ ] **Strong fencing,** because goats climb, squeeze, and jump: woven wire about 4-5 feet (1.2-1.5 meters) high, or electric netting, with no gaps. **Never barbed wire,** which injures them ([the barbed wire rule](06-outside-harassment-and-community-security.md) keeps livestock fencing separate). Movable electric netting lets them clear one patch at a time.
- [ ] **Keep them out of** the gardens, greenhouses, and orchards (they strip bark and kill young trees), the wash-and-pack station, the food store, drinking water, and the septic drain field, which their hooves compact ([Septic Systems](../../part-3-site-and-infrastructure/10-water-plumbing-and-septic/04-septic-systems.md))
- [ ] **Check the plants first.** Some common plants poison goats, such as rhododendron, azalea, mountain laurel, yew, and wilted cherry leaves. Ask the vet or the agricultural extension service for a local list, and remove or fence off anything dangerous.
- [ ] **Shelter** from rain, wind, and heat, clean water every day, hay when there isn't enough to browse, and minerals made for goats. Goat minerals contain copper that is poisonous to sheep, so keep them apart if both are kept.
- [ ] **Health:** a vet who treats goats, regular hoof trimming, and regular checks for worms, the most common goat health problem
- [ ] **A secure night pen** and the predator plan, with prevention first ([Firearms Safety](05-firearms-safety.md))
- [ ] **Milk, cheese, and meat** are regulated foods. None is sold or shared outside the household without following the food rules ([Chapter 6](../../part-2-market-engine/06-market-business-basics/README.md)).
- [ ] **Neighbors:** walk the fence weekly, since an escaped goat eats the neighbors' garden first, and check local limits on keeping goats
- [ ] **Children** may help with the goats under adult supervision, and wash their hands afterward

**Sheep for keeping the grass low (optional, under the animal rules above)**

Sheep are grazers. They prefer grass and low plants and crop them close, so they keep pasture, orchards, and open ground short and even. Goats and sheep work well together: sheep graze the grass, and goats browse the brush.

**Where they help most**

- Pasture, orchard floors under established trees, and the open ground between buildings
- The second way out, the fire breaks, and the helicopter landing spot, kept short ([Two Ways Out](../../part-3-site-and-infrastructure/12-roads-and-access.md#two-ways-out), [Remote Medical Plan](04-health-leadership-and-leaving.md#remote-medical-plan))
- Under solar panels, where sheep keep the grass down without climbing on the panels or chewing the wires, as goats might

**Two ways to use them**

- **Hire a grazing service,** whose owners bring the sheep, fencing, and water, on the same terms as hiring goats: any tier, with the landowner's permission, a written contract, and proof of insurance
- **Keep a small flock** under the animal rules above: Tier 3 or later, a group vote, a care lead and a backup, a daily rota, and a budget

**Keeping sheep**

- [ ] **Never just one.** Sheep are flock animals and panic alone. Start with at least three, such as ewes or wethers (neutered males). Any ram lives apart from the ewes (see **Breeding** above).
- [ ] **Consider hair sheep,** breeds that shed their coat and don't need shearing. Wool breeds must be sheared at least once a year, before hot weather, or they suffer from heat and flies.
- [ ] **Move them often.** Sheep graze close to the ground and can kill a pasture left too long. Rotate them through paddocks every few days, and let each one rest and regrow, which also cuts down worms.
- [ ] **Fencing:** woven wire about 4 feet (1.2 meters) high, or electric netting. **Never barbed wire,** which tears wool and skin.
- [ ] **Keep them out of** the gardens, greenhouses, young trees, the wash-and-pack station, the food store, drinking water, and the septic drain field ([Septic Systems](../../part-3-site-and-infrastructure/10-water-plumbing-and-septic/04-septic-systems.md))
- [ ] **Minerals made for sheep only.** Sheep are poisoned by small amounts of copper, so never give them goat minerals, cattle minerals, or feed with added copper. If goats and sheep share a pasture, put the goat minerals where sheep can't reach.
- [ ] **Check the plants first:** the same poisonous plants as for goats, such as rhododendron, azalea, mountain laurel, and yew. Bring sheep onto lush clover or alfalfa slowly, since a sudden feast can cause dangerous bloating.
- [ ] **Shade, shelter, and clean water every day,** thawed in freezing weather, and hay when the grass runs short
- [ ] **Health:** a vet who treats sheep, the vaccinations the vet recommends, regular hoof trimming (wet ground causes foot rot), worm checks, and, in warm weather, daily checks for flies laying eggs in soiled wool
- [ ] **Predators:** sheep can't defend themselves, so they need a secure night pen, and many flocks need a guardian animal, such as a guardian dog or a donkey (see **Guardian animals** above). Prevention comes first under the predator plan ([Firearms Safety](05-firearms-safety.md)).
- [ ] **Meat and milk** are regulated foods, under the food rules ([Chapter 6](../../part-2-market-engine/06-market-business-basics/README.md)). **Wool** from a wool breed can be spun, felted, or sold at the craft table.

**Chickens for eggs and pest control (optional, under the animal rules above)**

A small flock of hens gives the community eggs and eats insects, grubs, slugs, and weed seeds. Chickens are kept under the animal rules above: Tier 3 or later, a group vote, a care lead and a backup, a daily rota, and a budget.

**Eggs**

- **No rooster is needed** for hens to lay. Roosters are loud, and many places ban them. If a rooster is kept, he lives apart from the hens until the group decides to hatch chicks (see **Breeding** above).
- **Expect fewer eggs** in winter, while hens molt, and as they age. A hen lays well for a few years but can live for many more, so decide before starting what happens to hens that stop laying.
- **Collect eggs every day,** keep nests clean, throw out cracked or dirty eggs, and refrigerate eggs that are kept
- **Eggs are a regulated food.** The community's own share is fine, but eggs are sold or given outside the households only under the local egg rules, which often cover licensing, labeling, cleaning, and cold storage ([Chapter 6](../../part-2-market-engine/06-market-business-basics/01-getting-started.md))

**Pest control, without the damage**

Chickens scratch up seedlings, eat young plants and ripe fruit, and dust-bathe in soft beds. Put them to work where that doesn't matter:

- [ ] **In a garden bed before planting or after harvest,** to clear insects, grubs, and weed seeds, then out again
- [ ] **Under established fruit trees,** where they eat fallen fruit and the pests in it
- [ ] **In a movable coop or a pen of electric netting** (a chicken tractor), moved every few days so no patch is scratched bare
- [ ] **Out of the growing beds in season,** and away from the spider habitat the handbook protects, since chickens eat spiders too ([Chapter 15](../../part-3-site-and-infrastructure/15-porch-lights-and-garden-spiders.md))

**Keeping chickens**

- [ ] **Never just one.** Chickens are flock animals. Start small, for example with four to six hens.
- [ ] **A predator-proof coop:** hardware cloth (welded wire mesh), not chicken wire, which keeps hens in but not raccoons, foxes, or weasels out. A buried or turned-out apron of mesh, a locked door at dusk (an automatic door helps), and a covered run where hawks hunt.
- [ ] **Room to live:** roughly 4 square feet (0.4 square meters) per bird inside the coop and 10 square feet (1 square meter) per bird in the run, perches, about one nest box for every four hens, and ventilation without drafts
- [ ] **Where the coop goes:** away from drinking water, the wash-and-pack station, the food store, and the septic field, and downwind of homes
- [ ] **Food and water:** layer feed, crushed oyster shell for strong eggshells, grit, and clean water every day, thawed in freezing weather. Feed is stored in sealed metal bins, since it draws rodents.
- [ ] **Daily rota:** let the hens out, feed and water them, collect the eggs, and lock the coop at dusk. These are essential tasks, done every day, rest days included.
- [ ] **Hygiene:** everyone washes their hands after handling chickens, eggs, or the coop, since poultry can carry salmonella. Children under 5 don't handle chickens, and no poultry comes into homes or kitchens.
- [ ] **Bird flu:** keep wild birds away from the feed and water, use separate boots for the coop, and report sick or dead birds to the vet or the agriculture authority
- [ ] **Health:** a vet or extension adviser who knows poultry, and a check of the flock each day for injury or illness
- [ ] **Manure** composted before it goes near food crops, since fresh chicken manure is strong enough to burn plants and can carry germs ([Chapter 7](../../part-2-market-engine/07-production-planning/README.md))
- [ ] **Predators:** prevention first, under the predator plan ([Firearms Safety](05-firearms-safety.md))
- [ ] **Check local rules** on flock size, roosters, coop setbacks, and selling eggs

**Waste, trash, and recycling**

- Set up **trash, recycling, and compost** with clear bins and a collection plan
- Keep food waste in sealed bins, or in a compost system placed away from wells and the septic field ([Chapter 7](../../part-2-market-engine/07-production-planning/README.md))
- Never burn trash, and follow local rules for open fires
- Store hazardous waste (paint, fuel, chemicals) locked and away from food and children, and take it to a proper disposal site
- Do a **weekly walk-round** for litter and hazards

**Rest, joy, and wellbeing**

- **Schedule rest:** at least one day each week with no ordinary assigned work, for everyone, with essential tasks covered by rota
- Plan shared **meals, music, games, or celebrations**, so the community is more than a workplace
- Mark **harvests, new arrivals, and farewells** together
- Watch for loneliness, low mood, and burnout, and check in gently
- Make it easy to ask for help, and to say "I need a break"
- Keep a list of local support services, and know the crisis line

**Weekly rhythm** *(a sample to adapt)*

| Day | Example |
|---|---|
| Monday | Informal check-in (15-30 minutes, unrecorded), chore chart review |
| Tuesday-Wednesday | Garden work, repairs, seedling care |
| Thursday | Harvest, wash, and pack for market |
| Friday or Saturday | Market day, two-person cash count |
| Sunday | Rest day. Shared meal, quiet time. Essential tasks only, covered by rota. |
| Every day | Morning and evening greenhouse check |
| Monthly | Governance meeting in the Meeting Hall, budget review, food-store, greenhouse, and camera inspection, chore chart review |
| Each season | Review, open day, safety drill |

**Meetings that work**

- Keep the **weekly check-in short and informal**: what is going well, what needs help, and who is doing what. It is not recorded.
- Hold decisions, votes, and reviews in a **governance meeting** with an agenda, a timekeeper, and written minutes ([Chapter 2](../02-forming-a-group.md)), in the Meeting Hall once it exists
- Let everyone speak, including the quiet ones
- Settle small matters outside the meeting, and bring only shared decisions
- Never hold conflict hearings or personal criticism in an open meeting
