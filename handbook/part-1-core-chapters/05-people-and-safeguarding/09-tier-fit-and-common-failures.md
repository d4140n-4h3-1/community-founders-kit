# Tier Fit and Common Failures

## Tier Fit
- **Tier 0:** membership agreement, conduct rules, first-aid kit, one trained first-aider, posted emergency numbers, and a simple conflict process. Daily life basics: a chore list, weekly chart, the 12-hour guideline, an essential-task list, shared-space rules, quiet hours, and a weekly rest day. Security basics: boundary signs, a visitor sign-in, a named spokesperson, and the outside incident log.
- **Tier 1:** a trial period, two trained first-aiders, visitor rules, a safeguarding lead and deputy, and a disaster plan for the first season. Daily life: the weekly check-in, a tool sign-out list, a visitor welcome sheet, and food storage and waste routines. Security: a neighbor liaison and the response plan in the charter.
- **Tier 2:** written removal and appeal process reviewed by an attorney, mediation training for two members, fire drills, and a weekly check-in. Daily life: a shared kitchen with posted rules, a pet policy, a written meal-cost method, and the first open day. Security: locks and lights on key buildings, the camera policy adopted, and first cameras and signs installed. Meetings: the recording policy written and reviewed by an attorney, with consent added to the membership agreement.
- **Tier 3:** a board with non-residents hearing appeals, an independent route for complaints, and a succession plan for leaders. Daily life: the Lodge and the Meeting Hall with equal-booking policies, a bathhouse rota, a laundry schedule, volunteer days, and a monthly chart review. Security: more cameras with off-site storage. **Animals are optional and start at Tier 3 or later,** with a care lead, backup, and rota in place first.
- **Tier 4:** yearly review of conduct, safeguarding, emergency plans, security, camera logs, the meeting archive, and routines, and mentoring new groups on people rules and daily life

## Common Failures
- No written rules, so every problem becomes a personal argument
- Taking in anyone at once, with no trial period
- One founder holding all the power, money, and keys
- Hiding a safeguarding concern to protect the community's reputation
- Applying the rules to newcomers but not to old friends or leaders
- Removing someone with no process, then facing a legal claim
- Ignoring early signs of conflict until a group splits
- No first-aider on site, so a minor injury becomes a crisis
- Letting substance or weapons rules stay vague
- Burning out the one person who does everything
- Unwritten chores, so the same few people do everything
- Care work ignored, so cooks and carers burn out
- A work expectation worded as payment for housing
- Leaders exempt from the chore chart
- A dirty shared kitchen that becomes the main source of conflict
- No quiet hours, so tempers rise from lack of sleep
- No rest day, or a rest day that forgets essential tasks
- No one covering animals on rest days or when someone is ill (if animals are kept)
- Pets or animals loose around food and gardens
- Trash piled up, drawing pests (Chapter 15)
- Open meetings used to air personal complaints
- All work and no celebration, so people drift away
- Cameras used to watch residents instead of protecting shared places

## Money and Legal Tie-In
- Insurance may require safeguarding steps, background checks, security measures, and incident records (Chapter 4)
- Written agreements and removal steps must match local tenant and fee rules (Chapter 8)
- Training, first-aid supplies, mediation, shared food, waste, locks, signs, camera, and archive costs go in the expense log
- Record chore and volunteer hours (Appendix D7)
- Include safety incidents (without private details), drill dates, volunteer hours, and community days in the annual report

> Safeguarding, tenant, screening, recording, work-expectation, and mandatory-reporting rules differ widely by location. This handbook gives no legal advice, so have an attorney review the membership agreement, conduct rules, hours wording, camera policy, meeting recording policy, and removal process, and ask local services about training before anyone moves in. Keep routines simple and kind, and change anything that makes people dread the work.
