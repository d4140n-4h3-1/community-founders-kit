# Sister Villages

## Rule

**When more people want to join than the village can take in, the community chooses one of two paths:**

- **Grow larger,** if its resources truly allow it (see [Growing Larger](#growing-larger)), or
- **Start a sister village:** a new, separate village of about 20-30 people, with its own land, charter, money, and tiers, linked to the first by a written agreement.

Both are legitimate. The handbook is written for a village of about 20-30 residents ([Chapter 1](../part-1-core-chapters/01-introduction.md)), so a sister village lets every village keep working the way the handbook describes, while the wider community keeps growing.

This page is a bonus. It sits outside the tier triggers. A community adopts it by a vote when it first considers growing past its planned size, and records the decision in its [revision log](../../REVISION_LOG.md).

## Growing Larger or Starting a Sister Village

**"Resources allow" means more than money.** A village can grow larger when every one of these is ready, not just the bank balance:

| Resource | What growing larger takes |
|---|---|
| Water | Past a certain size, the water system becomes a regulated public water system. In the US, that usually means 25 or more people, or 15 or more connections. That brings required testing, a licensed operator, and reporting ([Chapter 10](../part-3-site-and-infrastructure/10-water-plumbing-and-septic/README.md)). |
| Wastewater | A shared septic system serving many people may be regulated as a commercial or large-capacity system, and may need an engineered system or a small treatment plant ([Septic Systems](../part-3-site-and-infrastructure/10-water-plumbing-and-septic/04-septic-systems.md)). |
| Land use | Many home sites on one property can count as a subdivision, with its own approvals, road standards, and fire-protection requirements ([Chapter 4](../part-1-core-chapters/04-legal-basics.md)). |
| Buildings | One Lodge and one Meeting Hall may no longer seat everyone, and larger halls face stricter assembly rules ([The Community Lodge](../part-3-site-and-infrastructure/09-shelter-evolution/05-the-community-lodge.md)). |
| Governance | Whole-group meetings and decisions by consent strain past a few dozen adults. A larger village needs representatives, such as neighborhood circles that send delegates to the governance meeting. |
| People | More trained first-aiders, mediators, and safeguarding leads, and more people ready to take on roles, so the load doesn't fall on the same few ([Leadership and Burnout](../part-1-core-chapters/05-people-and-safeguarding/04-health-leadership-and-leaving.md#leadership-and-burnout)). |
| Social fabric | Shared meals, the work guideline, and the conflict ladder rely on people knowing each other. That gets harder with every household. |

**How the two paths compare**

| | Grow larger | Start a sister village |
|---|---|---|
| Speed | New households join one neighborhood at a time | A new founding group starts at Tier 0, so it takes years |
| Regulation | Crosses thresholds for water, wastewater, and subdivision | Each village stays under them, if their systems are truly separate |
| Governance | Must change to representatives | Each village keeps whole-group meetings |
| Shared costs | One set of halls, roads, and systems | Two sets, though some equipment and services can be shared |
| Risk | One failure affects everyone | A bad season or a dispute in one village doesn't sink the other |
| Character | A small town | A cluster of villages |

**Choose to grow larger when** the community wants a town, and can meet every row of the first table. **Choose a sister village when** it wants to stay a village, or when any row isn't ready yet.

## Growing Larger

If the community chooses to grow larger:

- [ ] **Re-set the planned maximum size** by a vote, with the capacity check from [Chapter 13](../part-3-site-and-infrastructure/13-land-acquisition-and-expansion.md) done for the larger number
- [ ] **Grow one neighborhood at a time,** about 6-8 cabins, and finish each one's water, septic, roads, and capacity check before starting the next
- [ ] **Get the regulated systems in place first:** confirm whether the water system becomes a public water system and whether the wastewater system needs a new permit, and have the licensed operator or engineer ready before the population crosses the threshold
- [ ] **Get subdivision and land-use approvals** in writing before laying out new home sites
- [ ] **Change the governance before the size,** by charter amendment: for example, neighborhood circles that each send delegates to the governance meeting, with big decisions still going to everyone
- [ ] **Train more people:** a first-aider, a mediator, and a safeguarding contact for each neighborhood
- [ ] **Add a second equal hall** when the Lodge is regularly full ([The Community Lodge](../part-3-site-and-infrastructure/09-shelter-evolution/05-the-community-lodge.md))
- [ ] **Scale the money:** the six-month reserve, the property tax fund, and any lot fees recalculated for the larger number of planned lots ([Chapter 8](../part-2-market-engine/08-money-rules/README.md))
- [ ] **Have an attorney review** the whole plan, since a town-sized community may fall under rules this handbook doesn't cover

> This handbook is written for a village. A community that grows much larger is doing something the handbook has not been tested for, so plan carefully, take advice, and record what you learn in the revision log.

## When to Start a Sister Village

The first village, called here the **parent village**, is ready to help start a sister village when:

- [ ] It has reached **Tier 4**, with the six-month reserve intact and stable income ([Tier 4: Own](../part-4-tiers/tier-summaries/05-tier-4-own.md))
- [ ] Its **waiting list** is longer than it can take in within a year or two
- [ ] It has **two experienced members willing to mentor** the new group for at least its first two years
- [ ] It can do this **without weakening itself:** no critical role, cash, keys, filings, or safeguarding left without two trained people after anyone leaves to help found the sister village
- [ ] **Land** for the sister village is in sight (see [Land](#land))
- [ ] The parent village's members **vote** to go ahead, and its board approves

Helping a new group start at Tier 0 also meets the graduation condition in [Tier 4: Graduation](../part-4-tiers/unlock-triggers/05-tier-4-graduation.md).

## The Founding Group

- **Five to ten committed people,** as for any founding group ([Chapter 2](../part-1-core-chapters/02-forming-a-group.md))
- **Mostly new people,** often from the parent village's waiting list, plus **one to three experienced members** who choose to move. A mix carries the lessons without copying the parent's habits wholesale.
- Parent members who move **hand over their parent-village roles first,** with a written handover, so no role is left empty
- **No one leads both villages** at the same time
- Every founder goes through the honest conversation and signs a new founding agreement, as in [Chapter 2](../part-1-core-chapters/02-forming-a-group.md)

## Independence

**A sister village is not a branch.** It is its own community from the first day.

- [ ] **Its own founding agreement and charter,** adopted by its own members. It may start from the parent's charter, but votes on every rule.
- [ ] **Its own money:** its own two-signer account, budget, reserve, and records. No shared bank account.
- [ ] **Its own legal home:** its own fiscal sponsor, association, nonprofit, or land trust, as its tiers require
- [ ] **Its own tiers.** It starts at Tier 0 and meets every unlock trigger itself. The parent's records don't let it skip a tier.
- [ ] **Its own decisions.** The parent village advises, but never votes in, vetoes, or overrules the sister village's decisions.
- [ ] **Its own safeguarding,** with its own lead and deputy, and its own records

## Land

Choose one of these, with an attorney's help:

| Option | How it works | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| The sister village finds its own land | Like any new group: borrowed, leased, or donated land, then buying when it reaches Tier 4 | Slowest, but the most independent |
| The parent leases it part of the parent's land | A written [land-use agreement](../appendices/appendix-b-templates/b06-land-use-agreement.md) that meets the Tier 2 terms (at least 10 years before anything permanent is built), with a right of first refusal or purchase option | The parent must not be able to end it on short notice, or the sister village is never secure |
| A shared land trust | One land trust holds the land of both villages, with a separate long ground lease for each | Each village needs its own seats on the land trust's board |

**Keep the systems separate.** Each village has its own water source, septic system, and road access, on its own parcel or lease area. Ask the local authorities in writing whether two villages near each other will be treated as one system. If they are, the combined size may cross the thresholds the sister village was meant to avoid.

**Close enough to help, far enough to be separate.** Near enough to share a market stand, equipment, and help in an emergency, but with its own boundary, entrance, and neighbors.

## Money

- **A start-up gift from the parent's surplus,** never from its operating reserve, approved by the parent's board, including its non-resident members, and recorded in both villages' books ([Reserve and Inflation Protection](../part-2-market-engine/08-money-rules/02-reserve-and-inflation-protection.md))
- **Gifts and leases, not loans.** A loan the sister village must repay through a bad season puts both villages at risk. If a loan is made anyway, it is interest-free, in writing, and forgiven if the sister village steps back a tier.
- **Equipment** lent through a sign-out list, or given outright and recorded in the asset inventory of the village that owns it ([Appendix D8](../appendices/appendix-d-logs-sheets-and-reports/d08-asset-inventory-and-transaction-log.md))
- **Seedlings, seed, and surplus** from the parent's greenhouses for the sister village's first season
- **Every shared cost is split by a written formula** in the sister-village agreement, and recorded in both expense logs

## What the Villages Share

By written agreement, and only what both villages want:

- **The market:** a shared stand or alternating days, if the market's rules allow it, with each village's sales, cash counts, and sales tax kept separate ([Chapter 6](../part-2-market-engine/06-market-business-basics/README.md))
- **Bulk buying:** seed, gravel, block, and drywall bought together for a lower price
- **An equipment library:** larger tools and machines with a shared sign-out list and repair fund
- **Training:** first aid, mediation, safeguarding, hunter education, and firearms safety courses held together
- **Outside advisors:** a parent member can serve as the sister village's outside advisor until its Tier 3 board exists, if they live in the parent village and hold no role in the sister village ([Chapter 3](../part-1-core-chapters/03-charter-and-decision-making.md))
- **Appeals:** each village can name a member of the other as the independent person who hears appeals, which keeps them outside the dispute
- **Mutual aid:** each village's disaster plan includes the other, with a meeting place and a way to share shelter, water, and food in an emergency ([Appendix D15](../appendices/appendix-d-logs-sheets-and-reports/d15-disaster-plan-template.md))
- **A fiber link** between the villages' networks, with a firewall at each end, if both have adopted the [digital infrastructure bonus](digital-infrastructure.md#fiber-optic-network)
- **A yearly gathering** of both villages, to share lessons, celebrate, and review the agreement

## What Is Never Shared

- Bank accounts, reserves, and property tax funds
- Title to land, except through a shared land trust with separate ground leases
- Votes, charters, and memberships
- Safeguarding records, complaints, and members' private records
- Camera footage and meeting archives
- Water and septic systems

## Mentoring

- [ ] **Two named mentors** from the parent village, so the help doesn't depend on one person
- [ ] **Monthly check-ins** in the first year, then each season
- [ ] The parent shares its **revision log, budgets, crop plans, and lessons,** with private details removed
- [ ] Mentors join the sister village's tier reviews as advisors, using the [tier review worksheet](../appendices/appendix-e-tier-review-worksheet.md), but **don't vote**
- [ ] Mentors **advise, never decide,** and step back when the sister village reaches Tier 2, unless it asks them to stay
- [ ] The sister village writes its own lessons into the handbook, so the next group learns from both

## Tier Fit

- **Parent at Tier 3:** keep the waiting list and a land wish list, and note how many people the village can't take in
- **Parent at Tier 4:** choose between growing larger and a sister village, and, if a sister village, run the readiness check, vote, and sign the agreement
- **Sister village at Tier 0 and up:** follows the handbook from the beginning, with mentors, until it stands on its own
- **Both villages, every year:** a joint gathering and a review of the agreement

## Common Failures

- The parent village treating the sister village as its branch, and making its decisions
- The sister village skipping tiers because "the parent already proved it"
- Too many experienced members leaving at once, so the parent loses its trained people
- A founding group with no experienced members, so every lesson is learned the hard way again
- A shared bank account, or start-up money taken from the parent's operating reserve
- Two villages on one water or septic system, crossing the threshold they meant to avoid
- A lease the parent can end on short notice, so the sister village can't safely build
- Rivalry at the market, or one village always taking the better market days
- No way to end the agreement, so a strained relationship has nowhere to go
- Growing larger without changing the governance first, so meetings and decisions stop working

## Money and Legal Tie-In

- Record every gift, lease, shared cost, and piece of lent equipment in both villages' books
- Include the sister village, the support given, and what was learned in the parent's annual report ([Appendix D10](../appendices/appendix-d-logs-sheets-and-reports/d10-annual-report-template.md))
- Ask an accountant how a gift between two nonprofits or associations is treated, and whether it affects either's tax exemption

> Rules on public water systems, wastewater, subdivision, land trusts, and gifts between organizations differ widely by location. This handbook gives no legal advice, so have an attorney review the sister-village agreement, any land lease, and any plan to grow larger, and confirm with the local authorities how they will treat two nearby villages.
