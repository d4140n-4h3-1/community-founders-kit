# Taxes and Public Standing

**Principle:** **Pay what is owed, document what is exempt, and show the community's contribution openly.** Paying taxes won't stop critics from using labels like "freeloader," but taxes, fees, and transparency make the claim easy to disprove with facts. That protects the community's standing with neighbors, officials, and donors.

**What taxes and fees may apply**
| Item | Notes |
|---|---|
| Sales tax | Market sales often owe it. Register, collect, and remit correctly. |
| Property tax | Owned land pays it. Leased land usually passes it through the landowner. Nonprofit and land trust exemptions vary and often require an application and proof of public benefit. |
| Income, payroll, and self-employment | Depends on the structure and on whether anyone is paid |
| Permits and fees | Vendor permits, water, septic, and inspections are real contributions to the local system |
| Annual reports | Nonprofits often must file even when they owe nothing |

**An exemption is not freeloading**
Many places exempt nonprofits and land trusts on purpose, because they serve a public benefit. Critics may not know that, so plan for the perception:
- Pay what is owed without hunting for loopholes
- Where an exemption applies, document why the community qualifies and keep the filings public
- Never claim an exemption that has not been granted
- Don't assume an exemption carries over when land is bought, and confirm it before the purchase (see Chapter 13)

**Rules**
- [ ] Hire or consult an accountant **before the first market sale**
- [ ] Keep a **compliance calendar** of every tax, fee, permit renewal, and filing deadline, with two members responsible for it (no single point of failure, Appendix D9)
- [ ] Keep records for the period local law requires, and store copies off-site or in the cloud
- [ ] Separate community money from personal money, with a dedicated account
- [ ] Give receipts for sales and donations
- [ ] File on time, and correct mistakes quickly and openly
- [ ] Never pay or accept cash "off the books"

**Showing contribution beyond taxes**
- Publish a **short annual report**: income, spending, taxes and fees paid, food donated, volunteer hours, and what the community learned (Appendix D10)
- Sell at the local market, which feeds local business and pays vendor fees
- Donate surplus produce to a food bank, and record the amounts
- Buy locally, hire local trades for septic, electrical, and block work, and commission local artists
- Hold an open day for neighbors and officials
- Keep roads, ditches, and the property tidy, since appearance shapes opinion
- Name a neighbor liaison, and answer complaints in writing and promptly

**When someone calls the community freeloaders**
1. Stay calm and don't argue online
2. Share the facts: the annual report, tax filings, permits, and donation records
3. Invite them to visit or ask questions
4. Record the complaint and the response in the revision log and the outside incident log (Appendix D16)
5. If it becomes harassment or a threat, follow the outside harassment response plan (Chapter 5), document it, and involve the local authorities or an attorney

**Tier fit**
- **Tier 0:** consult an accountant before any sale, learn whether sales tax applies, register before the first taxable sale, and start the compliance calendar
- **Tier 1:** register for sales tax or permits as required, keep sales logs, and hold a dedicated account
- **Tier 2:** publish the first short annual report, and start a regular donation of surplus
- **Tier 3:** form the nonprofit or land trust, apply for any exemption, and document the basis for it
- **Tier 4:** audit filings and the report yearly, and publish both

**Common failures**
- Selling for a season before registering for sales tax
- Claiming a nonprofit exemption before it is granted
- One member handling every filing, then leaving
- Mixing personal and community money
- Missing a renewal, so a permit lapses
- Arguing with critics instead of showing records
- Keeping no records, then being unable to prove contribution

> Tax rules differ widely by country, state, and structure. This handbook gives no tax advice, so have an accountant confirm what applies to your community.
