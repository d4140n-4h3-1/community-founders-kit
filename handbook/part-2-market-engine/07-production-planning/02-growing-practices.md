# Growing Practices

## Succession and Seasons

- Sow small batches every 2-3 weeks for a steady supply, not one big flush
- Mix fast crops (radishes, greens) with slow crops (potatoes, squash)
- Match crops to the season: cool-season crops in spring and fall, heat-lovers in summer
- Extend the season with row covers, low tunnels, shade cloth, and the hoop house or greenhouse
- Build a local planting calendar from your frost dates and rainy seasons, not a generic one ([Appendix C](../../appendices/appendix-c-planting-and-market-calendar/README.md))
- Use the off-season for planning, seed orders, tool repair, and cover crops

## Soil, Compost, and Rotation

- **Feed the soil first:** compost, mulch, and cover crops (they also hold soil against wind)
- **Rotate crop families** so the same family does not follow itself in the same bed
- **Compost site:** away from wells, water, drainage paths, and the septic field, and out of reach of animals
- **Manure:** use only well-composted manure on food beds, and follow the local waiting period between application and harvest. Raw manure on food crops is a safety risk. Rabbit manure, if the community keeps rabbits, composts quickly and is gentle on plants ([Rabbits](../../part-1-core-chapters/05-people-and-safeguarding/16-rabbits.md)).
- **No treated or painted wood** in raised beds, and no sewage-based soil products unless local rules clearly allow them
- **Raised beds** near the water source suit Tier 0-1, with paths wide enough for carts

## Water for Crops

- Drip irrigation on timers, run early in the morning, with mulch to hold moisture
- Rain barrels and rainwater collection for beds (not for drinking, see [Chapter 10](../../part-3-site-and-infrastructure/10-water-plumbing-and-septic/README.md))
- Use **potable water** to wash produce. If irrigation water touches the edible part of a crop, check local food-safety rules and test the source if it is surface water.
- Check graywater rules before using it on any bed
- Record water use in the expense log
- **Bottle irrigation** for small beds and containers, especially before drip lines are affordable (see [Bottle Irrigation](#bottle-irrigation))

## Bottle Irrigation

**Rule:** at Tier 0 and up, reused plastic bottles can water plants slowly at the roots. Water seeps out through small holes into the soil, so less is lost to evaporation and runoff, and the plants need watering less often. It costs almost nothing and suits raised beds, containers, tomatoes, squash, peppers, and newly planted trees and bushes.

**Making and placing them**

- [ ] **Use drink bottles only,** about 1-2 liters (1-2 quarts), rinsed clean. Never use a bottle that held fuel, pesticides, cleaners, or other chemicals.
- [ ] **Make two to four small holes,** about 1-2 millimeters (1/16 inch) across, near the bottom of the bottle, with an awl or a heated nail. Test it before burying: it should empty over a day or two, not in minutes. Make the holes bigger or add more for sandy soil and hot weather, fewer for clay.
- [ ] **Bury it upright beside the plant,** about 2-4 inches (5-10 centimeters) from the stem, with the neck above the soil so it can be refilled. One bottle serves one large plant, or two or three small ones planted around it.
- [ ] **Or turn it upside down:** fill it, put on a cap with a few small holes, and push the neck into the soil next to the roots
- [ ] **Keep it capped or covered** between fillings, so dirt, leaves, and insects stay out and mosquitoes can't breed in the water ([Chapter 15](../../part-3-site-and-infrastructure/15-porch-lights-and-garden-spiders.md))

**Using them**

- [ ] **Fill through a funnel** every one to three days, more often in heat and wind, using rainwater or the same water as the rest of the beds. No graywater unless the local rules allow it.
- [ ] **Mulch over the soil** around the bottles to hold the moisture in
- [ ] **Check each week** for clogged holes, cracks, and bottles that empty too fast or not at all
- [ ] **Replace bottles each season,** before sunlight makes them brittle, so plastic doesn't break up in the soil. Recycle the old ones.
- [ ] **For a longer-lasting version,** bury unglazed clay pots (often called ollas) the same way. They cost more but last for years.

**Tier fit**

- **Tier 0-1:** bottles for the first beds and containers, filled by hand on the chore rota
- **Tier 1-2:** drip irrigation on timers takes over the market beds, and bottles stay useful for containers, new trees, and beds far from a hose
- **Tier 3 and up:** clay pots or drip lines for anything permanent

## Pests and Disease

Work in this order, and use the next step only when the one before fails:

1. **Prevention:** healthy soil, spacing, rotation, resistant varieties, and row cover
2. **Monitoring:** walk the beds weekly and note problems early
3. **Hand-picking, traps, and barriers**
4. **Beneficial insects and garden spiders** (protected under [Chapter 15](../../part-3-site-and-infrastructure/15-porch-lights-and-garden-spiders.md)), and, if the community keeps them, chickens in beds between crops ([Chickens](../../part-1-core-chapters/05-people-and-safeguarding/13-chickens.md)), guinea fowl for ticks and insects on pasture and field edges ([Guinea Fowl](../../part-1-core-chapters/05-people-and-safeguarding/14-guinea-fowl.md)), and ducks for slugs and snails ([Ducks](../../part-1-core-chapters/05-people-and-safeguarding/15-ducks.md)). Bees, kept or hosted, pollinate the crops ([Bees](../../part-1-core-chapters/05-people-and-safeguarding/17-bees.md)).
5. **Targeted organic spray as a last resort,** applied at dusk away from webs and logged

**Rules**

- No broad-spectrum pesticides in or near gardens
- Read and follow the label exactly, and wear the protective gear it requires
- Check whether a license is required to apply any product
- Store chemicals locked and away from the food store, the wash-and-pack station, greenhouses, living areas, and children ([Chapter 5](../../part-1-core-chapters/05-people-and-safeguarding/README.md))
- Do not describe produce as "organic" on signs unless local law allows it for your situation
- Use fencing and secure compost to deter wildlife
